## 1. DPIIT recognition

Issued by the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade under Startup India. An entity must meet every test:

| Test | Rule |
|---|---|
| Entity type | Private limited company, registered partnership firm, limited liability partnership (LLP) or cooperative society. Sole proprietorships, unregistered partnerships and public limited companies are not on the list. A one person company is not named: confirm on NSWS. |
| Age | Up to 10 years from the date of incorporation (20 years for DeepTech startups). |
| Turnover | Below ₹200 crore (₹300 crore for DeepTech) in every financial year since incorporation. |
| Innovation | Works towards innovation or improvement of products, services or processes, with potential to generate employment or create wealth. DPIIT judges this. |
| Original entity | Not formed by splitting up or reconstructing an existing business. |

- DeepTech status is DPIIT's decision. A company that passes only on the DeepTech limits is "needs checking".
- An entity stops counting as a startup once the age limit is reached, or once its turnover in a financial year goes over the limit (for exactly ₹200 crore, see section 12).
- Apply free on the National Single Window System (nsws.gov.in): Add Approvals > Central Approvals > "Registration as a Startup". File with the company's own email and mobile number. DPIIT charges no fee and appoints no agents.
- Certificates can be verified or downloaded on the Startup India portal or DigiLocker.
- What recognition unlocks (StartupReader DPIIT guide): self-certification under 6 labour laws and 3 environmental laws for 5 years; fast-tracked patent applications with an 80% rebate on filing fees and government-paid facilitators; winding up within 90 days under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 for simple debt structures; exemption from prior experience, turnover and earnest money deposit criteria in public procurement, and listing on GeM. It is also the entry ticket for 80-IAC, the Seed Fund Scheme, CGSS, funding from FFS-backed funds and convertible notes from foreign investors.

## 2. Section 80-IAC tax holiday (Section 140 of the Income-tax Act, 2025)

The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026. The startup deduction that was Section 80-IAC is now Section 140 ("Special provision in respect of specified business"). Startup India's pages still call it 80-IAC.

What it gives: a deduction of 100% of the profits and gains derived from the eligible business, for any three consecutive tax years out of ten years beginning from the year in which the start-up is incorporated (Section 140(1) and (2)).

Counting the ten years: the financial year of incorporation is year 1, so year 10 is nine financial years later. Incorporated on 12 August 2021: FY 2021-22 to FY 2030-31, and a three-year block can start in any of years 1 to 8. The deduction is worth nothing in a loss year, so the choice of block matters.

| Test | Rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Recognition | DPIIT-recognised first | Startup India 80-IAC page |
| Certificate | Holds a certificate of eligible business from the Inter-Ministerial Board of Certification (IMB) | Section 140(16)(b)(iii) |
| Entity type | Private limited company or LLP (the Act says "a company or a limited liability partnership"). Partnership firms and cooperative societies cannot claim it. | Startup India; Section 140(16)(b) |
| Incorporation | On or after 1 April 2016 and before 1 April 2030 | Section 140(16)(b)(i); IMB page |
| Age | Less than 10 years old when applying | Startup India 80-IAC page |
| Turnover | Less than ₹100 crore in any financial year (Startup India). The Act: total turnover does not exceed ₹100 crore in the year for which the deduction is claimed. | Startup India; Section 140(16)(b)(ii) |
| Business | Innovation, development or improvement of products, processes or services, or a scalable business model with high potential for employment generation or wealth creation | Section 140(16)(a) |
| Formation | Not formed by splitting up or reconstructing an existing business, nor by transferring previously used plant or machinery to a new business. Used plant up to 20% of the total value is allowed; machinery used only outside India, imported and never depreciated in India does not count as used. A business re-established after a natural calamity, riot, fire or enemy action can qualify. | Section 140(3) to (6) |
| Audit | Accounts of the eligible business audited for each year claimed, with the audit report furnished by the specified date | Section 140(8) |

- No DeepTech extension: neither the Act nor Startup India's 80-IAC criteria give DeepTech startups longer age or turnover limits.
- Turnover in practice: a year over ₹100 crore cannot be claimed, and a past year over it fails the Startup India application test. Exactly ₹100 crore is "needs checking" because the sources word the test differently.
- Scalability declaration (Startup India format): more than 10% revenue growth from one financial year to the next, or more than 25% cumulative growth over two financial years, or more than 33% over three. The script uses turnover as revenue.
- How the IMB evaluates: innovation grading (intellectual property, awards, product stage), scalability (the growth tests), wealth generation (funding, revenue, profitability) and employment (direct employment, diversity, staff in non-metro cities).
- Process: after recognition, log in to the Startup India portal with the same credentials, confirm the recognition details, fill in the 80-IAC details, upload documents, complete the scorecard, self-certify and submit. Track the status on the dashboard. StartupReader has no figure for how long the IMB takes.

## 3. ESOP tax deferral

- Normal rule: when an employee exercises options, the gap between the fair market value (FMV) on the exercise date and the price paid is taxed as salary (a perquisite), and the employer deducts the tax. On sale, the gain above that FMV is a capital gain, with the holding period counted from allotment. For unlisted shares, FMV is set by a merchant banker on the exercise date or a date up to 180 days earlier.
- Who can defer: only employees of an "eligible start-up as referred to in Section 80-IAC" (incometaxindia.gov.in), which means a start-up that meets the 80-IAC conditions, including the IMB certificate. DPIIT recognition alone is not enough. StartupReader's pages say "eligible DPIIT-recognised startups"; this is what "eligible" means.
- When the tax falls due under the 1961 Act text on incometaxindia.gov.in: the employer deducts it within 14 days of the earliest of (a) 48 months from the end of the assessment year in which the shares were allotted, (b) the employee leaving, (c) the employee selling the shares, at the rates for the year of allotment.
- From 1 April 2026: Section 392(3) of the Income-tax Act, 2025 keeps the deferral for "an eligible start-up referred to in section 140", with the tax deducted within the time specified in Section 289(3). Section 289(3) could not be read on the official site, so confirm the period for shares allotted from 1 April 2026 before stating it.
- The deferral moves the tax; it does not cancel it.

## 4. Angel tax

- "Angel tax" was Section 56(2)(viib) of the Income-tax Act, 1961: an unlisted company was taxed on share premium received above the fair market value of the shares.
- The July 2024 Budget announced its abolition for all classes of investors. The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 says the clause "shall not apply on or after the 1st day of April, 2025".
- Advisers read the cut-off differently for shares issued during 2024-25: if a round fell then, ask a tax adviser. The change was not retrospective, so earlier years can still be assessed under the old rule. Commentators note the Income-tax Act, 2025 has no equivalent provision.
- The old DPIIT exemption (February 2019 notification, Form 2) capped paid-up capital plus share premium after the issue at ₹25 crore, leaving out money from non-residents and venture capital funds. It matters only for old rounds.
- Still needed: a FEMA fair-value certificate for foreign investors, a registered valuer's report for preferential allotments, evidence of where money came from, and care with secondary sales below fair market value.

## 5. Startup India Seed Fund Scheme

StartupReader has no published guide to this scheme; these rules are from the scheme's own site (seedfund.startupindia.gov.in/about).

| Test | Rule |
|---|---|
| Recognition and age | DPIIT-recognised, and incorporated not more than 2 years before applying |
| Business | A product or service idea with market fit, viable commercialisation and scope of scaling, using technology in its core product, service, business model, distribution model or method |
| Other government money | Not more than ₹10 lakh of monetary support under any other central or state government scheme. Prize money, subsidised working space, founder monthly allowance and access to labs or prototyping facilities do not count. |
| Ownership | Indian promoters hold at least 51% when applying to the incubator |

- Money: up to ₹20 lakh as a grant for proof of concept, prototype development or product trials, paid in milestone instalments; up to ₹50 lakh for market entry, commercialisation or scaling through convertible debentures, debt or debt-linked instruments. Each form once.
- It is paid through incubators selected under the scheme, not by the government directly.

## 6. Credit Guarantee Scheme for Startups (CGSS)

- Borrower: DPIIT-recognised; not in default to any lending or investing institution and not a non-performing asset (NPA); eligibility certified by the lender (the member institution); a stable revenue stream, assessed from audited monthly statements over 12 months.
- Lenders: scheduled commercial banks and financial institutions; RBI-registered NBFCs rated BBB or above with a net worth of at least ₹100 crore; SEBI-registered Alternative Investment Funds. They register on the NCGTC portal.
- Loans covered: venture debt, working capital, subordinated debt, debentures, optionally convertible debt and other facilities that become debt obligations.
- Cover: at most ₹20 crore per borrower. Transaction-based cover is 85% of the amount in default for loans up to ₹10 crore and 75% above that. Umbrella-based cover for venture debt funds covers actual losses or 5% of the pooled investment, whichever is lower.
- Applying: through the Jan Samarth portal or a member institution's branch. The startup does not apply to the guarantor directly; the lender applies for the cover after sanctioning the loan.

## 7. Fund of Funds for Startups (FFS)

- A ₹10,000 crore fund launched in June 2016, managed by SIDBI for DPIIT. It invests in SEBI-registered Category I and II AIFs, which invest in startups.
- Founders cannot apply to FFS. They approach AIFs that have FFS commitments; the AIF makes its own decision. Funding from FFS-backed AIFs needs DPIIT recognition.
- StartupReader's FFS guide quotes an older DPIIT definition (₹100 crore turnover, no cooperative societies). Use section 1 for the current definition.

## 8. What changes with foreign investment

- Recognition and 80-IAC: the official criteria have no foreign-ownership condition. They apply to the Indian entity; a holding company abroad is not one of the listed entity types, so run the checks on the Indian company.
- Seed Fund Scheme: Indian promoters must hold at least 51% when applying, so a cap table with heavy foreign ownership can fail.
- Pricing: shares issued to a non-resident cannot be priced below fair value certified by a chartered accountant, a SEBI-registered merchant banker or a practising cost accountant. For convertible instruments the conversion formula is fixed at issue and the conversion price cannot be below the fair value at that time.
- Filings: issue the shares within 60 days of receiving the money (or refund within 15 days after that); Form FC-GPR on the RBI's FIRMS portal within 30 days of issue; Form FC-TRS for transfers between residents and non-residents; the annual FLA return by 15 July; Form DI within 30 days for downstream investment by a foreign-owned company.
- Routes: most sectors allow investment without prior approval (automatic route). Some sectors, and investors whose beneficial owners are in a country sharing a land border with India (Press Note 3 of 2020, clarified in March 2026 for non-controlling beneficial ownership up to 10%), need government approval.
- Instruments: equity shares, CCPS, CCDs and warrants. Convertible notes only from DPIIT-recognised startups: at least ₹25 lakh from an investor in a single tranche, converted or repaid within ten years, reported on Form CN within 30 days. US-style SAFEs are not listed under the FEMA rules.
- Angel tax no longer applies to new issues, but the FEMA valuation does.
- ESOPs granted by a US parent fall under US 409A rules; the Indian deferral in section 3 is for employees of an eligible Indian start-up.

## 9. Udyam (MSME) registration

- A separate registration under the MSMED Act, not the same as DPIIT recognition, and needed for MSME benefits. Registration on the official portal (udyamregistration.gov.in) is free, per StartupReader's Udyam guide.
- Classification since April 2025 (investment in plant and machinery or equipment, and turnover; exports excluded): micro up to ₹2.5 crore and ₹10 crore, small up to ₹25 crore and ₹100 crore, medium up to ₹125 crore and ₹500 crore.

## 10. Documents to prepare

- DPIIT recognition: the certificate of incorporation or registration, and a description of what the business does and how it innovates or scales. The exact list is on NSWS; StartupReader's guide does not reproduce it.
- 80-IAC (Startup India 80-IAC page): shareholding pattern as per the Memorandum of Association and the latest structure; board resolution, if any; income-tax return acknowledgements and audited financial statements (balance sheet and profit and loss) for the past three years or since incorporation, certified by a chartered accountant; a CA certificate that the startup was not formed by splitting up or reconstruction and that no previously used plant or machinery was transferred; the declaration of scalability; proof of credit rating, if any; proof of IP filings and awards; a pitch deck (PDF, up to 5 MB); an HR declaration (staff with an M.Tech or PhD, total direct employment, women, persons with disabilities, SC/ST staff, staff in non-metro cities); proof of investment received (term sheets, investment agreements, bank statements, investor certificates, GST filings).
  - Audited statements by age: 1 to 2 years old, the first year; 2 to 3 years, the last two; over 3 years, the latest three.
- Seed Fund Scheme: the DPIIT certificate, a CA certificate that the startup has not received more than ₹10 lakh under other government schemes, and the shareholding showing Indian promoters' share.
- CGSS: the DPIIT certificate and audited monthly statements covering 12 months, plus the lender's own list.
- ESOP deferral: the IMB certificate, a merchant banker valuation for each exercise, and a record of allotment dates.
- Foreign round: a fair-value certificate dated close to the issue, board and shareholder approvals, and Form FC-GPR.

Wording differences, treated as "needs checking" at the exact limit:
- DPIIT turnover: the official page says "less than ₹200 crore"; StartupReader's guide says "not exceed".
- 80-IAC turnover: Startup India says "less than ₹100 crore in any financial year"; its declaration and the Act say "not exceeded" or "does not exceed", and the Act applies the test to the year claimed.
- 80-IAC incorporation date: some pages say "after 1 April 2016"; the Act and the IMB page say "on or after".

Left out because no official or StartupReader page could confirm it: the DeepTech definition, the ESOP deferral period for shares allotted from 1 April 2026, how long the IMB takes, and whether a three-year block that began in a past year can still be claimed.
